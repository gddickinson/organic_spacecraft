I Structural Anatomy

Bark over bone over a breathing skin

The bulk scaffold is mycelium — the chitin-and-glucan hyphal networks already being pressed into fire-resistant building panels today, and studied under NASA's myco-architecture program. Mycelium is superb in tension yet weak under point compression — a mycelium composite holds only ~0.1–1 MPa in compression, even though its chitin nanofibrils reach ~1.5–3 GPa in tension — so it never works alone. Threaded through it is an osteoid lattice: cells analogous to osteoblasts lay down a collagen–hydroxyapatite matrix — literally bone, ~100–150 MPa in tension and ~150–200 MPa in compression — grown as an open trabecular foam rather than a solid, pairing cheap tension with the compressive strength mycelium lacks. Wolff's Law does the structural optimization for free: struts thicken where sensors report chronic load and are resorbed where they sit idle. The hull tunes its own skeleton at a resolution no engineer could draft by hand.

How it holds atmosphere against vacuum

The pneumostat membrane is not one skin but a stack of hundreds of thin, gas-tight lamellae — plywood, not cling-film. A puncture that vents one lamella is caught by the next; there is no single surface whose failure is catastrophic. The cabin runs at a deliberately low 52 kPa so hoop stress on the wall stays modest, and the organism spends metabolic energy staying sealed — scavenging back any gas that diffuses into the wall — the way your lungs actively manage gas rather than passively containing it. The load is quantitatively trivial: at 52 kPa the wall carries only ~0.65 MN per metre of hoop tension, which a few millimetres of silk-analogue fibre could hold — the full metre-thick pneumostat is a 15–300× margin, and the multi-metre wall exists for shielding, not for holding air.

A geometric note from the aerospace side: the pressure hull is a prolate spheroid, spin-decoupled from docking and habitat modules, because a rounded vessel minimizes wall stress per unit enclosed volume — the same reason cells, eggs and pressure tanks are round.

The materials, by the numbers

Real biopolymers already rival engineered ones. Mycelium composite (chitin + β-glucan, ~100–400 kg/m³) is light and cheap but weak in compression (~0.1–1 MPa)[7]. Trabecular bone (collagen + hydroxyapatite, Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂) carries ~150–200 MPa compression and ~100–150 MPa tension[6]. And spider-silk protein (~1–1.5 GPa tensile, toughness ~150–350 MJ/m³ — several times Kevlar's)[5] takes the concentrated hoop load. Each material does the one job it is best at; the hull is a composite in the literal biological sense.

II Metabolism & Life Support

The walls breathe for the crew

The inner lining is a dense sheet of engineered cyanobacteria and algae — think Synechocystis or Chlorella, the same organisms already run in closed-loop life-support testbeds. Given light, they consume crew CO₂ and release O₂, fixing carbon into sugars that feed the rest of the body. Because the entire inner surface is photosynthetic, the area-to-crew ratio is enormous; the limiting factor is light, not surface. Sunlight enters through clarified biocomposite "windows" on the star-facing hemisphere, supplemented by bioluminescent organs on the deep-space side so photosynthesis never fully stops.

How light crosses a metre of shield

The obvious objection: the intima sits behind a hull built for 305 g/cm² of radiation shielding — so how does sunlight reach it? The paradox dissolves because the shield's best material is water, and water is optically clear. That 305 g/cm² is just 3.05 m of water, and over that depth clear water still passes ~87% of blue-green light. A clarified water column in the star-facing "window" regions therefore does both jobs at once — it is the radiation shield and the light-pipe — while the opaque melanin rind, which nothing can see through, is reserved for the non-window areas.

One column, two jobs. Depth into the water is read two ways at once: as radiation shielding (amber, climbing to 305 g/cm²) and as an optical path. Blue-green light (530 nm) survives the full 3 m at ~87%; red (680 nm) is nearly gone at ~25%, so the intima is lit by an underwater field and its pigments are tuned like a deep-water cyanobacterium's — phycobilins, not a land plant's chlorophyll balance. The dark-side bioluminescent fill is real but only a trickle (biology tops out under ~1 W/m² vs the ~150 W/m² of useful daylight), so the sun-facing windows do essentially all the work. The habitat ARCA sidesteps the problem entirely: it pipes concentrated starlight down an axial sun-cord and lights its biomes from the inside, so its thick regolith shell never has to be transparent.

Why not light the whole inner wall? Because you don't need to — and it would cost you. Covering the crew's oxygen takes only ~1,400 m² of lit intima (≈18% of the 7,854 m² inner surface), ~2,500 m² with margin — roughly a third — so the intima is deliberately over-provisioned and only partly illuminated. Lighting more is not free: every window is a thinned patch in the radiation shield and adds heat the ship must dump, so the design captures the minimum light the O₂/food balance needs rather than maximising capture. When more of the intima should be productive — for food, margin or redundancy — light is distributed, not multiplied: grown transparent light-guides pipe window light to shaded tissue (ARCA's sun-cord trick, run internally); a slow roll lets the Sun sweep the whole hull over a "day" so every panel is lit in turn; and a scattering, reflective interior spreads what enters like an integrating sphere. The lever is always the same trade — window area vs shield integrity vs heat load — not raw collection.

Liquid or ice? Mostly it doesn't matter — clarity does. A window's phase follows its heat balance. A sun-lit window runs warm and liquid: water and ice are nearly opaque in the near-infrared, so it absorbs much of the ~720 W/m² of solar IR (the invisible half of the incident sunlight) while passing the visible through — enough to hold the column above freezing. A window on the dark side just radiates to space and freezes (~150 K), but it is lighting nothing anyway. So the natural state is a gradient — clear ice on the cold outer face, liquid deeper in — and it shifts as the ship turns. What matters is not the phase but the clarity: clear ice transmits visible light almost exactly like water (refractive index 1.31 vs 1.33; the same blue-green shift), because it is the same molecule. The enemy is scattering — bubbles, grain boundaries and micrometeoroid microcracks turn ice white the way they whiten snow. A frozen window therefore has to be grown clear (slow, degassed) and its cracks annealed or regrown, and it must run ~9% thicker than water for the same 305 g/cm² (ice is less dense but shields the same per unit mass). This is a quiet argument for a living window: an organism can degas, pump, keep the ice clear and heal its cracks, where a dumb block of ice would slowly frost over.

A materially-closed loop

Crew CO₂ → intima → O₂ + sugar. Solid and liquid waste feeds a gut-analogue of nitrifying bacteria that mineralize it back into nitrates and phosphates; urea becomes fertilizer for the intima. The design goal is that atoms cycle rather than deplete — resupply is needed only for what's lost to space and radiation breakdown.

The chemistry is exact. Photosynthesis runs 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ — by mass, 264 g of CO₂ and 108 g of water become 180 g of sugar and 192 g of oxygen. To remake the 0.84 kg of O₂ a crew member burns each day[1], the intima fixes ~1.16 kg of CO₂ and makes ~0.79 kg of sugar. The crew exhales only ~1.0 kg of CO₂, so the missing ~0.16 kg is supplied by the gut-analogue's decomposition — which is precisely how the loop closes on itself.

One honest wrinkle

Humans breathe with a respiratory quotient of ~0.87 (moles of CO₂ exhaled per mole of O₂ inhaled), while plants building sugar run a photosynthetic quotient near 1.0. That mismatch means CO₂ and O₂ do not balance one-for-one, so any closed ecology must actively buffer the difference — a real, measured headache in the MELiSSA and BIOS-3 closed-loop programmes[8][9][10], not something the biology solves for free.

Two interpenetrating tree-like networks distribute everything. A xylem-analogue carries water, minerals and oxygen-rich fluid outward to the growing tissues and bone-builders; a phloem-analogue carries the intima's sugars inward to every cell that can't photosynthesize. Flow is driven by slow, ship-wide peristaltic contractions — a metabolic heartbeat — plus osmotic gradients. The trunks run inside the osteoid struts, like marrow through bone, so the plumbing is armored by the structure it feeds. A central reservoir-and-liver organ banks water, lipids and a starch buffer so the ship can ride out sunless coast phases without starving.

III Space-Environment Defenses

Built to be hit, frozen, and irradiated

Radiation is met three ways at once. The melanized rind packs the same melanin that lets Cladosporium sphaerospermum — the "radiotrophic" fungus from inside the Chernobyl reactor, since tested on the ISS — absorb and dissipate ionizing radiation, arguably even harvesting a trickle of it as energy.[11] Behind it, hydrogen-rich water shielding is routed preferentially into whichever wall faces the highest particle flux. And because no shield stops galactic cosmic rays entirely, the cells carry Deinococcus radiodurans-style hyper-efficient DNA repair with redundant genome copies: the strategy is to out-heal damage, replacing expendable somatic cells from a shielded germline near the core.

Cold & micrometeoroids

In shadow, outer tissue drops into cryptobiosis like a tardigrade or wood frog, loaded with antifreeze glycoproteins (from Antarctic fish) and trehalose so no ice crystal can lance a membrane; metabolic heat from the core is circulated outward to thermoregulate. Micrometeoroids strike the sacrificial epidermis — a Whipple shield made of living, self-regrowing tissue, so craters heal instead of accumulating the way they do on a metal hull.

By the numbers. Thermally the ship is easier than intuition suggests: at 1 AU a dark hull settles near 278 K (5 °C) at radiative equilibrium[2] — an Earth-albedo hull ~255 K, a bright reflective one ~206 K — because the equilibrium tracks the absorb-to-emit ratio (α/ε) as its fourth root, so surface finish, not refrigeration, is the temperature lever, and the dark melanized rind conveniently pulls it warm. The crew's ~5 kW of metabolic heat needs only ~12 m² of ~290 K radiator to shed; against the ~2.7 MW of sunlight the hull intercepts and re-radiates, it is a rounding error. The real thermal work is at the extremes — deep-space coast, where the ship runs warm-blooded on metabolic heat behind heavy insulation, and close solar approaches, where it must dump megawatts.

Both extremes follow from one law: equilibrium temperature falls as the inverse square-root of distance (T ∝ 1/√r), so a fixed surface finish is only comfortable over a narrow ~0.2 AU-wide shell — about 0.8–1.0 AU for the dark hull. Living anywhere else means actively working the two levers the law leaves open: surface finish (α/ε) and radiators.

Two extremes, two strategies. Close in: at 0.3 AU a dark hull would reach 508 K (235 °C) and intercept ~30 MW of sunlight — survival means flipping the rind reflective (albedo ~0.9 drops absorbed heat to ~3 MW) and deploying radiators; even at 400 K, dumping 30 MW needs ~20,000 m² of radiator, larger than the hull, so close passes are brief, pale and radiator-heavy. Far out: sunlight — and with it photosynthesis — vanishes, so the ecology has no option but dormancy. A warm crew-core can still be held near 283 K on internal heat, but only behind heavy insulation: a bare hull radiates ~4.7 MW away, while multilayer insulation (ε≈0.02) cuts that ~50× to ~95 kW — within reach of metabolic and stored heat. The ship coasts cold and asleep, keeping just a warm heart beating.

Impact velocities run ~10–20 km/s (up to ~70 for retrograde dust). The epidermis works as a living Whipple bumper[14] — vaporising the projectile before it reaches the pressure wall — which cuts the required wall mass roughly ten-fold versus a single skin. Flux figures are order-of-magnitude (Grün interplanetary model[13]).

Why a thin living skin beats a thick one. A grain arriving at 10–70 km/s carries 50–2,450 MJ/kg — five to two-hundred times the ~10 MJ/kg it takes to vaporise rock — so it does not punch through the epidermis, it flashes to plasma on it (anything above ~4.5 km/s does). The resulting cloud fans across the standoff and delivers its momentum to the pressure wall spread over ~100× the area, so the wall survives with roughly a tenth the mass a monolithic shield would need. The living twist is that the cratered bumper simply regrows — the shield that heals is also the shield that never accumulates damage.

IV Growth & Maintenance

Grown from a seed, healed like a wound

The ship begins as a compact seed pod — a few hundred kilograms of totipotent stem cells, a stored genome, a starter nutrient charge and a photosynthetic culture, wrapped in a dormant, radiation-hard husk. That is all you launch. At a resource site it germinates: root-analogue mining hyphae dissolve regolith for calcium, phosphorus and metals, crack ice for water and oxygen, and a developmental program — chemical morphogen gradients and a segmentation-clock oscillator, exactly like an embryo[15] — tells each cell where to become hull, vascular trunk, or hollow habitat volume. Growth takes years, not hours; that is the central trade — you swap fast manufacturing for a vehicle that needs almost no launched mass and grows tougher over its life instead of wearing out.

Why "years, not hours"

The pace is set by mining and deposition, not by photosynthesis. Growing the reference 24,000-tonne starship in ~5 years means laying down roughly 13 tonnes a day — about 1.7 kg per m² of hull per day, or ~7 t/day of structure once the water is set aside. That is aggressive for biology (tens of times an alga's areal productivity), but the structure is assembled from mined feedstock delivered through the vasculature, and industrial bioleaching already handles tonnes per day — so the limit is how fast the ship can eat its asteroid, not how fast it can photosynthesise. Growth is exponential while the body is small, but the deposition rate is soon capped by that mining limit — so the rate plateaus rather than spiking, and gestation settles at ~5 years (see the growth curve below); run the deposition ten times slower and it simply stretches to ~50 years. The full mining chemistry and biomineralisation routes are set out in the Compendium.

Self-healing runs at three tiers. Micro-punctures clot in minutes with a fibrin-analogue plug — the same principle as self-healing bioconcrete — then regrow over days. A meteoroid gouge triggers a local growth surge that rebuilds the layers in order, seal first. And a catastrophic breach is survived by compartmentalization: sphincter-analogue bulkheads seal off a ruptured segment, sacrifice it, and regrow it from the boundary — the way a lizard drops and regrows a tail. Ongoing maintenance is mostly ensuring light, water, CO₂ and trace minerals, while a macrophage-analogue immune system polices infection and — the real design hazard — cancerous runaway growth.

Healing is not a separate system — it is the growth program aimed at a hole. The response is triaged by the size of the wound, escalating tier by tier, and every tier re-uses the same deposition-and-patterning machinery the organism grew with.

Seal fast, restore slowly. A breach triggers a wound signal (pressure drop + exposed matrix) that first drives a seal — a fibrin/calcite plug at millimetre scale, or a contracting sphincter bulkhead at metre scale — in seconds to minutes, because survival depends only on stopping the leak through the thin pressure layer. Restoring the full structure and shielding is the slow tail: stem/progenitor cells form a blastema at the wound, re-grow the vasculature into it (angiogenesis), and rebuild the six layers in order under morphogen gradients from the wound edge — a directed re-run of embryonic growth. At a local deposition surge of ~10–30× the baseline 1.7 kg/m²·day, the thin seal is rebuilt in hours, a half-metre gouge in ~1–3 weeks, and a full 5.5 m wall section in ~3–9 months — the deeper the wound, the longer, scaling with depth rather than area.

The growth engine behind all of it. Growth and healing share one front: mining roots feed ions up the vasculature to an advancing growth zone (a cambium- or blastema-analogue) where cells divide, differentiate, and secrete matrix — the ~13 t/day deposition of the gestation curve. Where each tissue forms is set by the morphogen-gradient and segmentation-clock circuits; whether it forms is gated by the very controls that prevent cancer — licence signals, Hayflick division caps, enforced apoptosis, and immune surveillance. Normal growth stops when the body reaches its target size (a size-sensing check, as organs stop growing at their set point); a wound simply re-opens that program in one place, and it closes again when the hole is filled. Healing is bounded growth; cancer is the same machinery with the brakes off — which is why the growth-control layers guard both.

The numbers land in a revealing place. Breathing and pressure-holding are comfortably solved — a third of the wall lit, a few millimetres of fibre to hold the air. What dominates the design is shielding mass: 24,000 tonnes of hydrogen-rich tissue, wildly impractical to launch, entirely practical to grow from the rock you parked next to. The concept earns its keep exactly where rockets fail.

Assumptions & caveats — O₂ at 0.84 kg/crew·day and ~30 g O₂/m²·day algal yield; pressure at 52 kPa held by silk-analogue fibre at ~100 MPa working stress (a ~10× margin on the fibre, and far more as built — the ~6.5 mm needed sits inside a metre-thick pneumostat); radiation from a ~0.6 Sv/yr deep-space GCR baseline attenuated with a secondary-particle floor (~0.35× residual). Galactic cosmic rays are genuinely hard to stop — the estimate is model-dependent and improves only slowly with more mass. These are first-order figures meant to test feasibility, not to size flight hardware.

Prove the bricks

Qualify each component alone — mycelium in vacuum, algal O₂ yield, bioconcrete healing, melanin dosimetry. Nearly all exists at bench scale today.

PHASE 1 · 5–15 yr
Culture the consortium

Build the synthetic lichen — fungus + alga + calcifier living as one composite — with a CRISPR toolkit and synthetic morphogen circuits for patterning.

PHASE 2 · 15–30 yr
Grow to shape & scale

Directed morphogenesis into metre-scale forms — first into molds, then self-guided. Gestate a small pressurized vessel on Earth and prove it holds air. The fully-gated, costed version of this roadmap is the Earth Program.

PHASE 3 · 30 yr +
Seed & gestate in space

Feed the organism real regolith and ice; qualify the dormant seed for launch. Germinate first in orbit, then at a resource body.

Two problems that will decide the whole program

Runaway growth. A ship that grows is a ship that can grow a tumour; enforced apoptosis and Hayflick-style division limits in somatic tissue are non-negotiable. Genetic stability. Over an organism's lifetime and its self-copies, mutation drift must not degrade the spec — which likely means error-correcting synthetic genomes, recoded for biocontainment, and a tightly policed germline[17][18]. Coordinating a three-partner consortium at stadium scale is the third hard bet.

Status of this document

A conceptual design dossier — a vision, not a build spec. The individual technologies cited (mycelium composites, radiotrophic melanin, antifreeze proteins, Deinococcus repair, self-healing bioconcrete, closed algal life support) are real and mostly demonstrated. Integrating them into a single coordinated multicellular organism the size of a stadium is far beyond current synthetic biology. The engineering figures are first-order estimates meant to test feasibility, not to size flight hardware.
