PLATE I

Section through a gestation cradle

VERTICAL SECTION · ~1 : 1,200
MID-TERM · NAVIS ~60% GROWN

A single cradle is a womb. Its wall is a placenta — a dense vascular lining fed by an umbilical trunk from the nursery spine — that bathes the growing ship in nutrient medium, lights it, and holds it to a shaping scaffold. Here a NAVIS starship is about 60% grown; controlled feeding, light and support roughly halve its wild gestation.

PLATE II

Nursery plan · the cradle frame

PLAN VIEW · ~1.2 km OVERALL

Cradles bud from a central feedstock spine like eggs on a fish's roe, gestating in parallel at every stage from freshly-seeded to hatching. Refined matter arrives from ATLAS at one end; finished vessels leave from the de-spun dock at the other.

PLATE III

Gestation sequence · seed to ship

FIVE STAGES · ~2–3 YR IN A CRADLE

A seed is loaded, the cradle envelops and connects its placenta, the ship differentiates and matures under fed, lit, scaffolded conditions, and the cradle opens to release it — commissioned in roughly half the time it would take to grow in the wild. This controlled seed-to-vessel gestation is exactly what the Earth Program's Phase 3 aims to demonstrate at metre scale on the ground, once the morphogenesis gate opens.

Drawing conventions

Green = living / photosynthetic tissue · cyan = engineered systems & data · amber = structure, fluid & shielding. Chain-dashed lines are centrelines; dashed lines are hidden edges. Each plate carries a title block (drawing no. · scale · revision); all sheets are CONCEPT — not for construction.

Why a nursery

A ship grown in the wild waits years on whatever an asteroid provides, laying down mass at ~13 t/day (see its growth curve). A cradle delivers concentrated nutrients, steady light and a shaping scaffold — roughly halving gestation, which means doubling the rate to ~26 t/day for the same 24,000 t NAVIS — and lets many hulls grow in parallel. It is how the fleet reproduces at scale.

The hard part

Each cradle grows a near-clone vessel it must feed without rejecting — so GRAVID borrows fetal–maternal immune tolerance[1], the real mechanism (regulatory T cells[2], the non-classical MHC molecule HLA-G[3], and a non-vascular placental barrier keeping the two circulations apart[4][5]) by which a mother carries a half-foreign fetus. The throughput itself is plumbing: ~26 t/day per cradle is ~0.3 kg/s of feedstock, delivered as a few litres a second of sap through a ~10 cm umbilical. The remaining hard parts are coordinating a dozen parallel morphogenesis programs[6][7], and biosecurity — a pathogen loose in a nursery reaches every hull it ships (see CORAL for the fleet's quarantine answer).

THE PUBLISHED BASIS

GRAVID's hardest problem — feeding and growing a near-clone vessel without rejecting it — borrows a mechanism biology already runs: how a mother carries a half-foreign fetus. That immunology, the placenta's transport role, and the morphogenesis circuits are all real and sourced here.

  1. Erlebacher, A. (2013). Immunology of the maternal–fetal interface. Annu. Rev. Immunol. 31, 387–411. — how the maternal immune system tolerates a semi-allogeneic fetus.
  2. Aluvihare, V. R., Kallikourdis, M. & Betz, A. G. (2004). Regulatory T cells mediate maternal tolerance to the fetus. Nat. Immunol. 5, 266–271. — Treg-mediated tolerance.
  3. Kovats, S. et al. (1990). A class I antigen, HLA-G, expressed in human trophoblasts. Science 248, 220–223. — the non-classical MHC molecule at the placental interface.
  4. Moffett, A. & Loke, C. (2006). Immunology of placentation in eutherian mammals. Nat. Rev. Immunol. 6, 584–594. — the placental barrier and maternal–fetal immune biology.
  5. Burton, G. J. & Fowden, A. L. (2015). The placenta: a multifaceted, transient organ. Philos. Trans. R. Soc. B 370, 20140066. — placental transport and vascular architecture (the cradle wall analogue).
  6. Toda, S. et al. (2018). Programming self-organizing multicellular structures with synthetic cell–cell signaling. Science 361, 156–162. — synNotch tissue patterning for directed growth.
  7. Elowitz, M. B. & Leibler, S. (2000). A synthetic oscillatory network of transcriptional regulators. Nature 403, 335–338. — genetic-oscillator basis for the parallel segmentation programs.
