Research · Development · Testing · Prototyping — the ground program

How You'd Actually Build It

A step-by-step program to develop the biology of GESTALT and grow the first prototypes on Earth — long before any seed is launched. Climbing the technology-readiness ladder, one honest gate at a time.

The concept documents describe a finished fleet. This one asks the only question that matters for making it real: what would you do on Monday? The answer is a decades-long research program that develops each biological subsystem to flight readiness on the ground, integrates them, and — if the hard part can be cracked — gestates a small living vessel in an Earth chamber before ever trusting one to space. It is written as a real roadmap: phases with entry and exit gates, work packages with lead questions, and a containment regime that comes first. Where the science is real it is marked; where it is a bet, the bet is named.

01 The shape of the program

Five phases up the readiness ladder

Each phase raises the technology-readiness level (TRL 1 = idea, 9 = flight-proven) and ends on a hard, falsifiable gate. Note the dip: the program cannot climb past Phase 2 until morphogenesis — the true long pole — catches up, however far ahead the other subsystems run.

Phase 0 · Prove the bricks (0–5 yr). Take each subsystem — mycelium composites[13], algal life support, self-healing bioconcrete[11], radiotrophic melanin, biomining — to reliable bench performance, in vacuum and thermal cycling where relevant. Almost all of this exists today; the work is qualification, not invention. exit gate each subsystem meets a written performance spec in isolation.

Phase 1 · Integrate (5–12 yr). Combine subsystems into single living artefacts: a structural panel that is also photosynthetic and self-healing; a closed nutrient loop fed by biomined feedstock. exit gate a self-supporting, self-repairing, oxygen-producing wall runs for a year.

Phase 2 · The morphogenesis moonshot (10–25 yr, the long pole). Move directed growth from millimetres to metres — patterned, layered, on command. This is the one subsystem starting at TRL ~2, and the whole program is gated on it. exit gate a seed grows a decimetre-to-metre patterned multi-tissue structure to a target design. This gate may never open; the honest program is designed to learn that as cheaply as possible.

Phase 3 · Gestate a demonstrator (25–40 yr). With patterning in hand, grow a metre-scale pressurised living vessel from a seed in an Earth chamber, fed on simulated regolith and ice. exit gate a seed becomes a pressure-holding, self-healing mini-hull.

Phase 4 · Space qualification (40+ yr). Fly the seed. Gestate a small vessel in orbit, then at a resource body. exit gate a launched seed grows a working vessel off Earth.

WP1 Structural biomaterials · detail

Growing a hull that holds up in vacuum

The bricks of the hull — mycelium scaffold, osteoid bone, silk tendon — mostly exist as terrestrial materials. WP1 qualifies them for space and pushes each to a hull-relevant spec. Three tracks, each with a rig and a number to beat.

The two hard sub-problems

Everything here is a known material at small scale; two things are genuinely hard. First, thick monolithic growth — mycelium is made in centimetre panels today, not metre blocks with uniform properties. Second, MICP to bone strength — microbial calcite reliably cements sand to a few tens of MPa, but reaching the ~150 MPa and the fracture toughness of real collagen-mineral bone needs the templated, layered deposition that only living cells do well. Both are integration problems, not new chemistry.

Gate (exit Phase 1): a self-supporting grown panel — mycelium scaffold + osteoid struts + a silk-cable edge — that survives thermal-vacuum cycling, holds a design load for a year, and is produced at ≥0.5 m scale. It feeds directly into the integrated living wall of WP2.

WP2 Closed-loop life support · detail

Closing the air and water loop, and keeping it closed

The individual reactions are demonstrated; the challenge is a living wall that runs for years without crashing. WP2 builds from a photosynthetic panel up to a sealed testbed that carries a real metabolic load.

The counterintuitive hard part

A small testbed is harder to keep stable than a whole habitat. The million-person ARCA has a ~140-year oxygen buffer in its ~113 Mt of air; a sealed room has almost none, so the RQ ≈ 0.87 / PQ ≈ 1.0 gas mismatch and every transient bite immediately — which is exactly how Biosphere-2's oxygen crashed. The research problem is therefore active dynamic control of a low-inertia closed ecology: buffering, sensing and correcting faster than it can drift. Solve it small and it only gets easier at scale.

Gate (exit Phase 1): a sealed system that keeps a crew-equivalent metabolic load in balance — O₂, CO₂, water and nitrogen — for a continuous year at >90% closure, integrated with a WP1 structural wall. This is the living intima of a real hull, proven on the ground.

WP3 Survival systems · detail

Self-healing, radiation-hard, and able to sleep

Each of these tricks is proven in some extremophile; WP3's job is to express them together in one structural, photosynthetic lineage — and against space conditions, not lab ones.

The integration problem

Individually these are real — self-healing bioconcrete is a commercial product, the tardigrade Dsup protein confers ~40% X-ray protection in human cells, and tardigrades have survived open space. The unsolved part is stacking a dozen traits into one lineage that must also be structural and photosynthetic, without them interfering. Note the neatest conflict and its resolution: radiation melanin is dark and would shade the intima — so the layered anatomy puts it in the outer rind and the photosynthesis on the inner wall, and the genome expresses each only where it belongs. That spatial regulation is, once more, a morphogenesis problem (WP4).

Gate (exit Phase 1): a single engineered lineage that self-seals a breach under vacuum, tolerates a kilogray, and revives from full cryptobiosis — the same cells that, in WP1 and WP2, also build structure and make oxygen.

WP4 The morphogenesis moonshot · critical path

The one bet everything waits on

Five of the six work packages qualify known biology. This one asks whether directed growth can climb ~14 orders of magnitude in cell number — from a millimetre organoid to a kilometre organism. It is the gate on the whole program, and it is deliberately built to be tested early and failed fast.

Rung 21 dm · ~10¹²Decimetre construct: a correctly layered wall and a branching vascular tree feeding itmake-or-break — vasculature must scale with tissue
Rung 3 Phase 2 gate1 m · ~10¹⁵A metre mini-hull grown from a seed — layered, vascularised, holding pressurereached ⇒ concept credible; not ⇒ program stops here
(Beyond)10s of m · ~10¹⁹⁻²⁰A full small vesselPhase 3, only after Rung 3

The circuit stack to break the millimetre barrier

A single morphogen gradient reaches only ~1 mm, so the program builds a hierarchical patterning stack: axis gradients (French-flag) to set the body's poles; a segmentation clock[9]-and-wavefront — an oscillator laying repeating units one at a time as the tissue grows, exactly as vertebrate somites form — to pattern beyond gradient range; synNotch contact circuits to build the radial wall layers; Turing reaction–diffusion for the fine texture; and stress-directed deposition with enforced apoptosis to shape and bound the growth.

The real wall is plumbing, not patterning

Oxygen and nutrients diffuse only ~100–200 µm into tissue before cells starve — which is why every capillary in your body is about that far from the next. So any living structure thicker than a fraction of a millimetre needs internal vasculature, and it must grow in step with the tissue it feeds. This, not raw cell number, is what separates today's millimetre organoids (avascular, necrotic in the middle) from a metre of living hull. Self-organising vasculature at scale is the true long pole inside the long pole — and Rung 2 is exactly where the program finds out whether it exists.

Fail-fast by design. The rungs are ordered so the hardest new capability — a vascularised decimetre construct — is reached in the first ~10–15 years, not the last. If Rung 2 cannot be made to work, the honest program concludes that grown kilometre-scale organisms are not achievable with foreseeable biology and says so, having spent a fraction of the full cost to learn it. And every other work package still yields real materials, closed life-support and biomining technology regardless.

WP5 ISRU, biomining & the seed · detail

Turning a rock into feedstock — and packing it into grams

GESTALT's whole leverage — a half-tonne seed becoming a 24,000-tonne ship — rests on two capabilities: eating a resource body for its elements, and shipping the organism as a dormant package that revives on arrival. WP5 builds both on regolith and ice simulants.

Biomine metalsAcidithiobacillus[18] + phosphate-solubilisers on regolith simulant (JSC-1A, MGS-1)Useful Fe/Ca leaching rates; concentrate the scarce P from ~0.1% feed (the ISRU bottleneck)5→6
Process volatilesIce-simulant melt + electrolysis (~15.9 MJ/kg water); CO₂ / organics captureWater, O₂ and carbon feedstock at target purity from a dirty ice analogue4→6

Real, and already off Earth

The mining half is the program's most flight-proven piece: ESA's BioRock[17] experiment (ISS, 2019–20) showed microbes leaching rare-earth elements from basalt in microgravity. The open problems are autonomy and the phosphorus concentration — a starship needs ~440 t of P, so at ~0.1% feed it must process ~18× its own mass in rock, unsupervised, for years. The seed half is the leverage itself: across the fleet, seeds run from ~2 kg (CHORUS) to ~500 kg (NAVIS), and the entire ~48,000:1 grown-to-launched ratio depends on one reviving reliably after transit.

Gate (exit Phase 1–2): a benchtop loop that feeds a growing culture on nothing but processed regolith and ice simulant, and a dormant seed that survives a simulated interplanetary transit and germinates. Together they close the "grows from local resources" premise on the ground.

WP6 Genome, safety & containment · detail

The package that has to work before any of the others count

WP6 is numbered last and scheduled first. The moment an engineered lineage is asked to grow, self-repair and reproduce, its containment is no longer a compliance afterthought — it is the enabling technology. Every gram of biomass any other work package produces is only permitted because WP6 has shown that biomass cannot survive outside the rig, cannot swap genes with wild life, and can be switched off. So this package leads: it is the one whose exit criteria gate entry to every escalation everywhere else in the program.

Layered kill-switchesTwo orthogonal inducible-lethality circuits (toxin–antitoxin[5] "deadman" + "passcode" logic) that require a continuous benign signal to stay aliveReliable induced kill on signal-withdrawal; combined mutational escape below the auxotrophy floor after long passage3→5

Why this one goes first — and it is largely real

Every ingredient exists. Recoded genomes are built: E. coli C321.ΔA[1] has all 321 of its UAG stop codons reassigned (Lajoie et al., 2013), and the rE.coli-57[2] project set out to free seven codons genome-wide (Ostrov/Church, 2016). Built on that, synthetic auxotrophy already reaches an escape frequency below 10⁻¹² — undetectable in trillions of cells (Mandell & Rovner[3][4] et al., Nature 2015). Kill-switches with "deadman" and "passcode" logic are demonstrated devices (Collins lab, 2016). What WP6 adds is not new science but stacking these into one lineage that is simultaneously structural, photosynthetic and radiation-hard — and proving the safety loci survive the very genome-copying a self-growing organism depends on. That last row is the honest weak point: containment that must hold across a vessel's lifetime of cell divisions is closer to TRL 2 than the single-generation lab demonstrations, and it is the reason nothing in this program ever plans an environmental release.

Gate (leads all phases): a single lineage that is synthetically auxotrophic below 10⁻¹¹ escape, phage- and HGT-resistant, killable on demand by two orthogonal switches, and stable in all four properties over a demonstrator's worth of generations — signed off by the independent board. Until a construct clears this bar it stays in its current containment tier; WP1–WP5 escalate only behind it. This is the ground rehearsal for the flight-side Governance & containment regime, and it applies the genetic-safety toolkit detailed in the Compendium.

03 How the packages converge

Six work packages, one climbing artefact

The work packages run in parallel, but they are not the deliverable. The deliverable is a single living thing that does all six jobs at once — and it is built by making that thing bigger and more integrated one rung at a time. Each rung folds in another package and roughly a thousand-fold more cells; the pips show how many of the six subsystems must co-exist in one lineage before that rung counts as passed.

Where the ladder can break — and it is the same place every time

Rungs 1 and 2 are integration engineering: hard, but every ingredient is demonstrated, and a working panel is a genuine product even if the program stops there. The wall is the step from panel to closed module — a flat sheet is not a pressure vessel, and turning a growing tissue into a sealed, curved, load-bearing shell is precisely the morphogenesis problem (WP4). That single transition, marked amber, is why the phase ladder dips at Phase 2: rungs 1–2 can be reached in the first decade with today's biology, but rung 3 waits on the one capability nobody has yet. The mass figures are deliberately modest — a ~2.4 t demonstrator is smaller than the fleet's smallest flight vessel — because the point of the ground program is to prove the mechanism, not the scale; the ~48,000:1 grown-to-launched leverage is a flight-side reward, not a thing to chase in a chamber.

The convergence rule: a rung is passed only when a single lineage clears it with all of that rung's subsystems co-expressed and its WP6 containment intact at the new scale — never by bolting separately-grown parts together. Each rung is a checkpoint on the five-phase ladder above, and the whole climb is the same story the fleet tells at flight scale: the Compendium's cell-count ladder, walked one contained step at a time.

04 Containment first

The safety case leads the science

A program to build self-reproducing engineered organisms cannot bolt on safety at the end. Every phase runs under an escalating containment regime, and WP6 gates the others: no organism graduates to a larger or less-contained stage until its biocontainment is demonstrated.

The rule

Work proceeds only inside physical containment (BSL-appropriate labs and closed bioreactors) and genetic containment (synthetic auxotrophy for a non-natural nutrient, recoded genomes that cannot exchange DNA with wild life, and inducible kill-switches) — with an independent biosafety and ethics board holding a standing veto. Environmental release is never a research step; it is a separate, later, publicly-deliberated decision. Nothing in this program requires releasing anything, ever, on Earth.

This mirrors how recombinant DNA was actually governed after Asilomar (1975): capability and containment developed together, with the second leading. GESTALT's stakes are higher, so the discipline must be stricter — see the Governance & containment section of the fleet registry for the flight-side controls this ground regime is the rehearsal for.

05 Time, money & people

Four decades, a national-lab's budget, and one bet that dominates it

Stripped to a schedule, the program is six work packages laid across ~40 years. Five of them are ordinary — well-scoped bench-to-pilot biology that would be worth funding on its own. One, morphogenesis, is the critical path: it starts early, runs almost the whole length, and every later milestone waits on it. Containment (WP6) is the other full-length bar — not because it is slow, but because it must lead every escalation.

What it would cost

These are order-of-magnitude figures — the honest precision at this stage is one significant figure. They assume a distributed, publicly-funded effort at academic-plus-national-lab scale, and they deliberately exclude Phase 4 (spaceflight), whose cost is launch-dominated and separate.

Is ~$30–40B a lot? For a 40-year program, no.

Averaged over its life that is ~$0.8B/yr — the running cost of a single national laboratory, and about 3–4% of one space agency's annual budget. For comparison: the Human Genome Project ran ~$3B, JWST ~$10B, ITER ~$45B. GESTALT's ground program is roughly one JWST per decade — unremarkable for a civilisation-scale capability, and back-loaded so that the expensive phases only begin after the cheap early science has shown the hard bet can be won. If morphogenesis fails at Phase 2 (~$15B, ~year 25), the program stops there having spent a third of the total and delivered real materials, closed-life-support and biomining technology as spin-offs. That is the design: fail cheap, before the costly chamber-and-gestation phase.

Who builds it, and where

Tier 1 · Distributed labs

Phases 0–1. Existing university and institute benches (BSL-1/2) running the six work packages in parallel — synthetic biology, materials, closed-ecology. No new facility; the work is qualification, and its spin-offs justify it alone.

Tier 2 · Morphogenesis Institute

Phase 2, the moonshot's home. A dedicated biofoundry (automated strain design–build–test at scale) plus imaging and organoid-to-tissue rigs, aimed at the one capability nobody has. This is where most of the money and most of the risk live.

Tier 3 · Integration & gestation facility

Phases 3+. Thermal-vacuum chambers, pilot bioreactors, a closed-ecology testbed, and finally a large sealed, containment-hardened growth chamber with a regolith/ice-simulant processing plant — where a seed becomes a metre-scale vessel.

Standing · Independent oversight

Across all tiers, the WP6 biosafety & ethics board with a real veto, plus institutional biosafety committees. This is the flight-side Governance regime, rehearsed on the ground.

Read the chart as one sentence: spend a little to prove the bricks, a little more to integrate them, then make the one big bet on directed growth — and only if it pays does the program graduate to the chamber that gestates a living vessel. Every horizontal bar is governed, top to bottom, by the containment bar beneath it.

06 What this program is

A way to find out — cheaply — whether it can be done

Mostly de-risking known biology

Five of six work packages are qualification of demonstrated science (TRL 3–5). That half is a normal, fundable engineering-biology program — worth doing for its own spin-offs (materials, closed life support, biomining) whether or not a spacecraft ever grows.

One real bet

Morphogenesis at metre-to-kilometre scale (WP4) is TRL ~2 and may prove intractable. The program is deliberately structured to reach that gate early and fail fast if it must, rather than spend decades assuming it will open.

Decades and billions

Order-of-magnitude: ~40 years and tens of billions of dollars to a space-gestated vessel — comparable to fusion (ITER ~$25B) or a genome-scale program — and only if the moonshot lands. This is a civilisation-scale research bet, framed honestly as one.

The point of writing it as a real program is not to promise the fleet. It is to show that the path from today's laboratory to a living starship is a finite, structured sequence of experiments with clear gates — most of them fundable now — and to name exactly where that path might end. The sections above fill in each stream; the gates below say how it ends.

07 Decision gates & synthesis

Every phase is allowed to say no

A program that could run 40 years and cost tens of billions has to be honest about how it ends — including badly. GESTALT's answer is that each phase turns on a single falsifiable question, and a clear "no" stops the work. The gates are ordered so the cheapest questions are asked first and the one most likely to fail — morphogenesis — is asked before the expensive chamber phase. Nothing here is open-ended; the whole thing is a sequence of yes/no experiments.

The design goal in one number: 45%

The gate most likely to fail is Phase 2, and it lands at ~45% of total spend — after which the program either graduates to gestation or stops, having answered whether directed growth can climb ~14 orders of magnitude in cell number. Either outcome is a result. That is the whole philosophy: buy the answer to the hardest question before paying for the chamber that assumes the answer is yes.

What would actually make us stop

1 · Morphogenesis plateaus

The likeliest failure: directed growth cannot be pushed past ~cm of patterned tissue because vasculature won't co-scale with the structure. This is the WP4 long pole and the Phase-2 gate.

2 · The traits won't share a cell

A dozen engineered functions — structural, photosynthetic, radiation-hard, self-healing — interfere irreducibly when stacked in one lineage, and no spatial regulation resolves it.

3 · Containment can't hold at scale

The WP6 safety loci mutate out over the generations a growing vessel needs. Unlike the others, this "no" halts the program on principle, not just feasibility — and it is checked first.

4 · The ecology won't stay closed

The low-inertia closed loop cannot be stabilised even with active control, so life support can't run for years without resupply — the lesson BIOS-3 and Biosphere-2 already taught, unbeaten.

5 · It grows too slowly to matter

The gestation-rate math says a useful vessel takes impractically long to grow from a seed, removing the point even if every other gate opens.

The oversight that never lapses

Across all seven sections, one thing is constant: the WP6 biosafety & ethics board holds a standing veto, environmental release is never a research step but a separate publicly-deliberated decision, and any defensive-class design in the fleet (e.g. TESTUDO) stays strictly defensive — the program builds shelter, not weapons. This ground regime is the rehearsal for the flight-side Governance & containment controls the fleet will need.

The whole program, in one breath

The concept documents describe a finished fleet — starships, million-person habitats, surface colonies, nurseries — grown from seeds. This program is the root of that tree: a finite, gated, mostly-fundable sequence that develops each biological subsystem to readiness, integrates them into one living artefact, makes a single honest bet on directed growth, and — only if that bet pays — gestates a small living vessel in an Earth chamber. It shares one cell-count ladder with the fleet, walked one contained rung at a time. Whether it ends at a starship or at a pile of genuinely useful spin-off technology, it is designed to find out cheaply, safely, and in the open which of those it will be. That is the most that can honestly be promised — and it is enough to begin.
