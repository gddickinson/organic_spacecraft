Metabolic Physiology · Nutrition & Waste

Rock In, Ship Out

A grown vessel makes its body from a carbonaceous asteroid and a star. Here is how it ingests rock and light, digests them into building blocks, metabolises those into tissue, and — recycling almost everything — excretes little but heat and spoil.

Every other GESTALT document treats the vessel as already grown. This one asks the animal question: how does it make a living? A grown ship has two mouths — a root that takes in rock and ice, and a skin that takes in light — and its whole physiology is organised around a startling fact established below: photosynthesis on the hull can only make about a quarter of a tonne of biomass a day, but the ship grows at thirteen. So the intima's photosynthesis is a life-support organ that makes the crew's air; the body itself is grown by eating the rock — and, as §05 shows, the rock supplies the matter far more readily than the energy. What follows is the alimentary physiology in four acts — ingest, digest, metabolise, excrete — grounded in a mass-and-energy budget. Real, demonstrated chemistry is marked green; the integration is marked gap.

01 The strategy

Two mouths, almost no waste

An animal eats, digests, metabolises and excretes across an open gut: matter flows through it. A spacecraft cannot afford that — in the desert of space, everything thrown away is gone for good. So GESTALT metabolism is built on a single principle: take in only what cannot be recycled, and excrete only what physics forbids you to keep. Two things must be taken in continuously — fresh matter (you are building a body from nothing) and fresh energy. Two things must inevitably leave — waste heat (the second law is not negotiable) and inert rock spoil (you cannot use all of an asteroid). Almost everything in between runs in closed loops.

The whole-body flow. Two intakes (light, rock & ice) cross the boundary; two wastes (heat, tailings) leave it. Carbon, water and nitrogen turn in closed internal loops — only leakage needs topping up. The dashed return is the closure that a spacecraft lives or dies by.

The headline number

Grown-vessel biology is often imagined as "a big plant." It is not. To photosynthesise its own 13 t/day of growth, a NAVIS hull would need ~440,000 m² of lit intima — about 33× its entire outer surface. It has nowhere near that. The intima instead runs at life-support scale (a few thousand m², making the crew's oxygen). The body is grown by mining and eating the rock, which is why growth is set by the digging rate, not the sunshine — provided the ship can find the energy to build with, which the rock alone cannot give it. §05 does the arithmetic.

02 Ingestion

Rock, ice and light

The vessel ingests through the RADIX mining root, which anchors to a resource body (a carbonaceous asteroid, a moon, or planetary regolith) and takes matter in by three methods, and through the skin and clarified windows, which take in light. The choice of body matters: a carbonaceous asteroid is not just ore — it is food, carrying up to a few per cent reduced organic carbon left from the birth of the solar system, plus water ice.[1]

The two mouths. The root ingests solids by mechanical boring, chemical bioleaching (the main route) and thermal volatile release;[11] the skin ingests light. Between them they bring in minerals, water, carbon and nitrogen — and the energy to use them.

Ingestion is selective: chemoreceptors in the root read the ore and steer the growing tip toward richer seams,[2] exactly as a plant root grows toward nutrients. See the Cell Atlas resource cells for the cell types that do the work.

03 Digestion

The mineral gut

Rock is not food until it is broken into ions and molecules the tissue can absorb. The root is therefore a digestive tract: a sequence of compartments at different pH and redox, each extracting a fraction, exactly like a stomach and intestine.[3] The undigested residue leaves as tailings (§06).

The alimentary canal, from mouth to spoil. Acid leaching is the "gastric" step; a separation gut absorbs the wanted ions; a volatile refinery splits water and handles carbon and nitrogen; the chondritic organics are hydrolysed into the actual food. Everything the body cannot use is voided as inert tailings.

The gastric step — bioleaching

Acidophile cells oxidise iron and sulfur to generate sulfuric acid in situ — real, industrial biomining chemistry[3] — and the acid dissolves the surrounding minerals:

2 FeS₂ + 7 O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 FeSO₄ + 2 H₂SO₄ (acid generation)
H₂SO₄ + silicates/carbonates → metal²⁺ ions + CO₂ + H₂O (dissolution → a lixiviant "chyme")
on Mars: ClO₄⁻ (perchlorate) → Cl⁻ + O₂ (detox — and a bonus of oxygen)[4]

04 Circulation & absorption

The bloodstream

Digested feedstock is useless where it is made; it has to reach 1019 cells, none of them more than ~200 µm from a supply (the Krogh limit).[12] GESTALT solves this the way trees and animals do — a branching vascular tree obeying Murray's law, carrying two saps in opposite directions.

Down-sap · phloem-analogue

Sugars and amino acids flow out from the intima and the gut to the growing tissue — the delivery of food and building blocks, pushed by contractile pericytes.

Up-sap · xylem-analogue

Water and dissolved minerals flow in from the root; some conduit cells die to become hollow pipes, exactly as xylem does.

Return · waste pickup

The same network carries spent CO₂, water and nitrogen back toward the intima and the recyclers, so the loops can close (§06).

The plumbing is sized like the nursery's umbilicals: on the order of ~3 L/s of nutrient sap through a ~10 cm trunk feeds a whole vessel-scale organism.[8] The cell types that build and pump it are catalogued in the Cell Atlas.

05 Metabolism

Two engines, and the budget that proves it

Here is the crux of the whole physiology. GESTALT runs two metabolisms at once, and confusing them is the commonest mistake about grown vessels.

Engine 1 · phototrophy → the air

The intima runs oxygenic photosynthesis: CO₂ + H₂O + light → sugar + O₂. Sized for life support — it makes the crew's oxygen and some food, on a few thousand m² of lit surface.

Engine 2 · chemotrophy → the body

The bulk of the body is grown by eating the rock: its reduced organic carbon supplies building blocks and, burned with an oxidant, energy. But an asteroid is a fuel with almost nothing to burn it with — the catch this section quantifies.

Why it must be this way — the energy & area budget

The intima produces biomass at roughly the rate of a good algal culture, ~30 g/m²·day[9] — about 0.4% of the sunlight on it. Multiply that by every square metre of lining and you get a number that settles the argument:

The budget that forces the design. Left: even with its whole ~9,000 m² lining lit, the intima makes ~0.25 t/day of biomass — growth is ~13 t/day, so ~98% of the body cannot be photosynthetic; matching it with light would take ~33× the hull's outer area. Right: building 13 t/day stores ~0.8–2.6 MW in new tissue and, at roughly half efficiency, needs ~1.7–5.4 MW. The rock carries ~2 MW of organic fuel but little to burn it with: its ferric iron, perhaps a little sulfate and the intima's spare O₂ unlock only ~0.2–0.5 MW. Sunlight on the hull (2.7 MW pole-on, 5.3 broadside) is mostly reflected and never enters this account.

The catch — a fuel with no oxidiser

A carbonaceous asteroid carries organics but almost nothing to burn them with. Releasing the ~2 MW in them would take ~14 t of O₂ a day; the intima, lit wall to wall, spares ~0.2 t after the crew. Making the rest by electrolysis would cost ~2.8 MW — more than burning it returns. What the rock does hold is ferric iron (magnetite, ferrihydrite, Fe(III)-bearing clays) and perhaps a little sulfate, which anaerobic microbes use as electron acceptors in place of O₂ — iron reduction of the Geobacter kind[14], and sulfate reduction. If a quarter to a half of a CI chondrite's ~18 wt% iron is ferric[16] (an assumption), at ~240 t/day of ore these unlock ~0.2–0.5 MW, with Fe³⁺ worked in the acid gut at close to its best (~0.75 V per electron); sulfate yields a tenth as much per electron. That powers only ~0.5–3.5 t/day of growth — 20–130 years for a 24,000 t hull. Growth is energy-limited, and the Dossier's ~5-year gestation needs energy from outside.

The resolution — and the biggest bet

The only outside source big enough is the Sun — not through the intima, which stores ~0.4% of the light it gets, but through an electro-organ: a sunward collector feeding electrons to electrotrophic microbes that fix CO₂ into feedstock (microbial electrosynthesis). A "bionic leaf" of this kind turns electricity into bacterial biomass at ~50% efficiency, and sunlight at ~10% when paired with ordinary solar cells[15]. At 10%, the missing ~0.6–2.5 MW of new tissue needs ~6–25 MW of sunlight: ~4,300–19,000 m² of collector, up to 1.4× the hull's own area. No such organ is in the drawings, and none has ever been grown. A grown ship is a chemoheterotroph that happens to breathe — its carbon and bulk matter arrive through the root — but its building energy must, in the end, come from the star. Photosynthesis is the lung, not the stomach; the rock is a larder without a stove.

Anabolism — turning food into tissue

sugars + amino acids + Ca²⁺/PO₄³⁻ + Fe/trace → chitin (mycelium) · collagen & hydroxyapatite (bone) · lipids & protein (membranes) · melanin (rind) → deposited along stress lines as the hull grows

Maintenance is cheap by comparison: the crew burns ~5 kW, and 24,000 t of mostly low-metabolism structural tissue idles at perhaps ~0.1 MW basal[13] — orders of magnitude below the growth engine, which is why a finished ship costs far less to run than to build (see sustainability). Deep-space classes, past ~3 AU where light thins, must carry stored energy or piped light for even the life-support engine — and cannot grow at all without it. gap

06 Excretion

The four waste streams

A well-closed organism is defined by how little it throws away. GESTALT has only four exits, and two of them barely count.

Four exits, ranked by how much truly leaves. Only heat is unavoidably lost (the second law); tailings are the big mass stream but are re-used, not dumped; carbon/water/nitrogen are recycled and only leak; toxics are locked away. The interesting engineering is how small the true output is.

The one-line physiology

Rock and light in; tissue built; heat and spoil out; everything else goes round again. A grown ship is closer to a coral reef or a soil than to a rocket — a slow, near-closed chemistry that only vents what it thermodynamically must.

07 The whole budget

Mass and energy, in and out

Putting the four acts together for a NAVIS growing at ~13 t/day. Every figure is order-of-magnitude and grade-dependent; the point is the shape of the budget, not three significant figures. The mass ledger is set by phosphorus: the ship's ~2,400 t of bone mineral holds ~440 t of P, and at ~0.1% P in the ore that means processing ~18× the ship's mass in rock — the same ratio the Compendium and the Earth Program use.

08 What is real, and what is a bet

Every step here is real chemistry — bioleaching, meteoritic organics as a carbon-and-energy source, water electrolysis and Sabatier, radiative cooling, Murray's-law circulation, biomineralisation. That is the green. The bets are, as always, in the integration and the rate:

Bet 1 · the living refinery

Running an entire ore-to-tissue processing chain as one living organ, continuously, at ~240 t/day, is far beyond any demonstrated bioreactor. gap

Bet 2 · the grade & the logistics

The ~18:1 ore-to-ship ratio, set by phosphorus, means the root must chew and void enormous masses; the numbers assume a rich carbonaceous body close at hand. gap

Bet 3 · the efficiencies

Photosynthetic yield, extraction fractions and synthesis costs are all taken optimistic; a factor of a few either way moves the whole timeline. gap

Bet 4 · the energy import

The rock is a fuel without an oxidiser; growing at ~13 t/day needs ~1–5 MW from outside. The candidate — a sunward electro-organ feeding microbial electrosynthesis at ~10% — exists as a benchtop bionic leaf, not as anything grown, and it is not in the drawings. This is the hardest metabolic bet in the program. gap

The honest summary: each reaction here is demonstrated, and the mass ledger closes. The energy ledger closes only with an import: on its own chemistry an asteroid-eating ship can power a few tonnes a day of growth at most, so the 13 t/day of the growth curve depends on bringing in megawatts of sunlight-derived energy — a bet, not a result. Making any of it happen inside one coordinated body, at rate, is the enterprise the Earth Program exists to test. This is a concept study, not a build spec.
