Metabolic Physiology · Nutrition & Waste

Rock In, Ship Out

A grown vessel makes its body from a carbonaceous asteroid and a star. Here is how it ingests rock and light, digests them into building blocks, metabolises those into tissue, and — recycling almost everything — excretes little but heat and spoil.

Every other GESTALT document treats the vessel as already grown. This one asks the animal question: how does it make a living? A grown ship has two mouths — a root that takes in rock and ice, and a skin that takes in light — and its whole physiology is organised around a startling fact established below: photosynthesis on the hull can only make about half a tonne of biomass a day, but the ship grows at thirteen. So the intima's photosynthesis is a life-support organ that makes the crew's air; the body itself is grown by eating the rock. What follows is the alimentary physiology in four acts — ingest, digest, metabolise, excrete — grounded in a mass-and-energy budget. Real, demonstrated chemistry is marked green; the integration is marked gap.

01 The strategy

Two mouths, almost no waste

An animal eats, digests, metabolises and excretes across an open gut: matter flows through it. A spacecraft cannot afford that — in the desert of space, everything thrown away is gone for good. So GESTALT metabolism is built on a single principle: take in only what cannot be recycled, and excrete only what physics forbids you to keep. Two things must be taken in continuously — fresh matter (you are building a body from nothing) and fresh energy. Two things must inevitably leave — waste heat (the second law is not negotiable) and inert rock spoil (you cannot use all of an asteroid). Almost everything in between runs in closed loops.

The whole-body flow. Two intakes (light, rock & ice) cross the boundary; two wastes (heat, tailings) leave it. Carbon, water and nitrogen turn in closed internal loops — only leakage needs topping up. The dashed return is the closure that a spacecraft lives or dies by.

The headline number

Grown-vessel biology is often imagined as "a big plant." It is not. To photosynthesise its own 13 t/day of growth, a NAVIS hull would need ~430,000 m² of lit intima — about 29× its entire surface. It has nowhere near that. The intima instead runs at life-support scale (a few thousand m², making the crew's oxygen). The body is grown by mining and eating the rock, which is why growth is set by the digging rate, not the sunshine. §05 does the arithmetic.

02 Ingestion

Rock, ice and light

The vessel ingests through the RADIX mining root, which anchors to a resource body (a carbonaceous asteroid, a moon, or planetary regolith) and takes matter in by three methods, and through the skin and clarified windows, which take in light. The choice of body matters: a carbonaceous asteroid is not just ore — it is food, carrying up to a few per cent reduced organic carbon left from the birth of the solar system, plus water ice.[1]

The two mouths. The root ingests solids by mechanical boring, chemical bioleaching (the main route) and thermal volatile release;[11] the skin ingests light. Between them they bring in minerals, water, carbon and nitrogen — and the energy to use them.

03 Digestion

The mineral gut

Rock is not food until it is broken into ions and molecules the tissue can absorb. The root is therefore a digestive tract: a sequence of compartments at different pH and redox, each extracting a fraction, exactly like a stomach and intestine.[3] The undigested residue leaves as tailings (§06).

The alimentary canal, from mouth to spoil. Acid leaching is the "gastric" step; a separation gut absorbs the wanted ions; a volatile refinery splits water and handles carbon and nitrogen; the chondritic organics are hydrolysed into the actual food. Everything the body cannot use is voided as inert tailings.

The gastric step — bioleaching

Acidophile cells oxidise iron and sulfur to generate sulfuric acid in situ — real, industrial biomining chemistry[3] — and the acid dissolves the surrounding minerals:

2 FeS₂ + 7 O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 FeSO₄ + 2 H₂SO₄ (acid generation)
H₂SO₄ + silicates/carbonates → metal²⁺ ions + CO₂ + H₂O (dissolution → a lixiviant "chyme")
on Mars: ClO₄⁻ (perchlorate) → Cl⁻ + O₂ (detox — and a bonus of oxygen)[4]

04 Circulation & absorption

The bloodstream

Digested feedstock is useless where it is made; it has to reach 1019 cells, none of them more than ~200 µm from a supply (the Krogh limit).[12] GESTALT solves this the way trees and animals do — a branching vascular tree obeying Murray's law, carrying two saps in opposite directions.

Down-sap · phloem-analogue

Sugars and amino acids flow out from the intima and the gut to the growing tissue — the delivery of food and building blocks, pushed by contractile pericytes.

Up-sap · xylem-analogue

Water and dissolved minerals flow in from the root; some conduit cells die to become hollow pipes, exactly as xylem does.

Return · waste pickup

The same network carries spent CO₂, water and nitrogen back toward the intima and the recyclers, so the loops can close (§06).

The plumbing is sized like the nursery's umbilicals: on the order of ~3 L/s of nutrient sap through a ~10 cm trunk feeds a whole vessel-scale organism.[8] The cell types that build and pump it are catalogued in the Cell Atlas.

05 Metabolism

Two engines, and the budget that proves it

Here is the crux of the whole physiology. GESTALT runs two metabolisms at once, and confusing them is the commonest mistake about grown vessels.

Engine 1 · phototrophy → the air

The intima runs oxygenic photosynthesis: CO₂ + H₂O + light → sugar + O₂. Sized for life support — it makes the crew's oxygen and some food, on a few thousand m² of lit surface.

Engine 2 · chemotrophy → the body

The bulk of the body is grown by eating the rock: digesting its reduced organic carbon and oxidising its reduced minerals (Fe, S) with the intima's oxygen, releasing the energy and carbon to build tissue.

Why it must be this way — the energy & area budget

The intima produces biomass at roughly the rate of a good algal culture, ~30 g/m²·day.[9] Multiply that by every square metre of hull and you get a number that settles the argument:

The budget that forces the design. Left: the intima on the whole hull makes ~0.45 t/day of biomass — but growth is 13 t/day, so ~97% of the body cannot be photosynthetic; matching it with light would take 29× the hull area. Right: the ~0.8–2.6 MW to synthesise 13 t/day comes from the feedstock's own organic chemical energy (~1.4 MW) plus mineral oxidation — sunlight (6.4 MW intercepted) is mostly reflected for thermal control and never enters this account.

The resolution

A grown ship is a chemoheterotroph that happens to breathe. Its carbon, its building energy and its bulk matter all arrive through the root, from the asteroid — which is exactly why the Dossier's growth curve is mining-limited, not light-limited. Photosynthesis is the lung, not the stomach.

Anabolism — turning food into tissue

sugars + amino acids + Ca²⁺/PO₄³⁻ + Fe/trace → chitin (mycelium) · collagen & hydroxyapatite (bone) · lipids & protein (membranes) · melanin (rind) → deposited along stress lines as the hull grows

Maintenance is cheap by comparison: the crew burns ~5 kW, and 24,000 t of mostly low-metabolism structural tissue idles at perhaps ~0.1 MW basal[13] — orders of magnitude below the growth engine, which is why a finished ship costs far less to run than to build (see sustainability). Deep-space classes, past ~3 AU where light thins, must carry stored energy or piped light for even the life-support engine. gap

06 Excretion

The four waste streams

A well-closed organism is defined by how little it throws away. GESTALT has only four exits, and two of them barely count.

Four exits, ranked by how much truly leaves. Only heat is unavoidably lost (the second law); tailings are the big mass stream but are re-used, not dumped; carbon/water/nitrogen are recycled and only leak; toxics are locked away. The interesting engineering is how small the true output is.

The one-line physiology

Rock and light in; tissue built; heat and spoil out; everything else goes round again. A grown ship is closer to a coral reef or a soil than to a rocket — a slow, near-closed chemistry that only vents what it thermodynamically must.

08 What is real, and what is a bet

Every step here is real chemistry — bioleaching, meteoritic organics as a carbon-and-energy source, water electrolysis and Sabatier, radiative cooling, Murray's-law circulation, biomineralisation. That is the green. The bets are, as always, in the integration and the rate:

Bet 1 · the living refinery

Running an entire ore-to-tissue processing chain as one living organ, continuously, at ~100 t/day, is far beyond any demonstrated bioreactor. gap

Bet 2 · the grade & the logistics

The ~9:1 ore-to-ship ratio means the root must chew and void enormous masses; the numbers assume a rich carbonaceous body close at hand. gap

Bet 3 · the efficiencies

Photosynthetic yield, extraction fractions and synthesis costs are all taken optimistic; a factor of a few either way moves the whole timeline. gap

The honest summary: the metabolism is thermodynamically sound and each reaction is demonstrated — a grown ship eating an asteroid breaks no laws. Making it happen inside one coordinated body, at rate, is the enterprise the Earth Program exists to test.
